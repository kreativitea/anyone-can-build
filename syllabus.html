Anyone Can Build: The Art of Product Engineering with AI

Kansai Gaidai University · Asian Studies Program · Fall 2026 Fridays, approximately 3:00–6:00 PM (two periods) · Nakamiya Campus, Room 3411 · Instructor: Michael Omoto

An introduction to AI-native software engineering for students with no prior programming experience.

The exact order of dates, readings, guests, and topics may change to accommodate schedules.

What This Course Is

Just as Chef Gusteau insisted that "anyone can cook," this course is built on the conviction that anyone can build. If you have never written a line of code, you are exactly who this course is for.

You will step into the role of a software engineer and discover that the job is far more than writing code: it is a way of delivering products that actually work, for real people, at scale and over time. From the first week you will work the way professional engineers now do — with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) as your tool. Over the term you take one real idea of your own from a rough concept to a working product, deployed to a live URL that anyone can visit.

You will not build the "toy programs" of a typical intro course. You will build something genuinely useful, and you will learn to direct the AI while keeping the important decisions yours. This course grades what you understand and decide — never the code the AI produced.

What You Will Be Able to Do by the End

  1. Take an idea from a real problem all the way to a working minimum viable product (MVP).
  2. Direct an AI coding assistant as a collaborator to plan, build, and debug — while the design decisions stay yours.
  3. Read, question, and debug AI-generated code instead of accepting it on faith.
  4. Experience the full reality of engineering work, including the debugging and the maintenance.
  5. Judge a product for quality, maintainability, security, and ethics — not just whether it runs.
  6. Leave with an honest sense of how large this field is, and a concrete first step into it.

What to Expect

No prior programming experience is required or expected. (If you have programmed before, you are welcome too, and you will help as a peer mentor.)

The course meets Friday afternoons in two consecutive periods. It is build-first and project-based. Bring a laptop. You will need a paid Claude Code or Codex account to do the work; if you do not have one on the first day, you can use Antigravity for free to get started.

You will use an AI coding assistant set to a learning mode that explains its reasoning and asks you to write key pieces yourself. Across the term you build one real product, deploy it live, and defend your understanding of it.

Because AI can make a beginner feel more capable than they really are, your understanding is checked directly and often. You pass by showing what you understand and own — explaining your product, fixing a real bug live, justifying your decisions — not by the size of what the AI produced.

One practical note: your work is sent to a third-party AI service. Handle it in line with Japan's personal-data rules (APPI), and keep personal or sensitive data out of it.

The weekly journal. The last five minutes of every Friday, from Week 2 on, are for reflection. You write one dated entry in your tracker repository — the SDLC stage you are in, what you did this week, what you learned, and what you want to carry forward — and commit it before you leave. You may ask the AI to list what you worked on; what you learned and what you carry forward are in your own words.

What it takes to pass (comprehension). Because AI can produce a polished artifact a beginner cannot yet explain, passing requires you to demonstrate four core competencies, each checked inside the graded components above:

  • (a) explain how any part of your product works;
  • (b) implement a new feature in an in-class practical;
  • (c) justify your architecture and your build-vs-buy decisions;
  • (d) show your tests catching a real change.

Grades above the pass line reflect the defended depth of extensions to the same product — not the number of features. This is a competency requirement, not an attendance or submission rule.

Your final (in place of a written exam). The semester-long project is your final, evaluated in the exam period (Dec 10–16) on the quality of what you built rather than its size:

  • (a) a coherent, well-designed architecture that holds together — the parts fit, patterns and names are consistent, and there is no contradictory or dead structure;
  • (b) the security standards from Weeks 11–12, met and demonstrable: correct authentication and per-user access control (Supabase row-level security), input validation, secrets kept in environment variables and never in code, HTTPS, no open items among the OWASP Top Ten basics, and APPI-appropriate handling of any personal data;
  • (c) ideally, deployed to a public URL and usable by real people in a small beta — not only on your own machine.

The comprehension requirement above still gates the grade: the project counts as yours only if you can explain and defend it.

There is no written final exam and no attendance points. You get written feedback on every deliverable.

Textbooks and Tools

There is no required textbook; all materials are provided. Your reference materials are the official documentation for the course tools — Claude Code, Supabase, Sentry, Mixpanel, and Cloudflare Pages / GitHub Pages — plus readings assigned along the way. These specific tools are representative industry examples chosen for their capable free tiers and good documentation, not endorsements; any comparable tool would serve.
