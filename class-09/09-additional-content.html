Class 9 — Additional content

Assigned after class, on top of the syllabus outside-work: do it before next week. It takes about forty-five minutes. This week's main homework, adding likes and follows to Timeline, is on the Class 10 page, so this one is short.

Do

  • If you had no problem on Friday, start with the Class 8 page. The problem card in its four parts, one interview with a real person, and the press release. Next week you write a design doc for your problem, so it comes first.
  • Paste your Timeline model into your tracker, as you and your agent grew it. Four tables at least: users, posts, follows and likes. Every table has its columns, every link is an ID used in another table, and every name is made up. Mark each of the four changes: did it need a new table, or not? Keep visible what your agent changed.
  • Write three sentences in your tracker. Which change spread through your model on Friday, what you changed instead, and why the new version holds. Use the test from the session: when something changes, how much else has to change?

Read

  • Robert C. Martin, Screaming Architecture (2011; ~2 min: the first two paragraphs, the house and the library; free). A programmer reads two building plans by their rooms and knows at once what each building is for, the way the class read three blueprints. The architecture would scream: house means it shows plainly that it is a house. A foyer is an entrance hall, and a dinette a small eating area. The rest of the post is about code you have not written yet; stop at the question So what does the architecture of your application scream?
  • Martin Fowler, Who Needs an Architect? (2003; ~5 min: the section Getting rid of software architecture, from that heading to the end; free PDF). A well-known software architect on why architecture is "things that people perceive as hard to change", with a colleague's comparison: "It is hard to go back and change your basement". It is the class's test, when something changes, how much else has to change?, from the other side. A database schema is the data model: the tables and their columns. Skip the paragraph that begins My reservation of Aspect-Oriented Programming. The last line, We have met the enemy, and he is us, means the limit is people, not the material.
  • IBM, What is three-tier architecture? (~6 min: stop at Other multitier architectures; free). The three tiers from Friday, as the industry describes them. It is a company's page, written partly to sell cloud services, but its definitions are the standard ones. Two things to notice. All communication goes through the middle tier, the application tier: the frontend never talks to the database directly. And a layer is not quite a tier: a tier is a layer that runs on its own machine, so the Contacts app on your phone has three layers and one tier. One warning: this page calls the data tier "the back-end", while most tutorials use that word for the server. Both are in use.
  • Julia Evans, anatomy of a HTTP request and anatomy of a HTTP response (2019; ~2 min: one drawn page each; free to view). The request and the response from Friday's network panel, with each part labelled by hand. The method is what the request asks for, such as GET to read or POST to send; headers are extra notes on the message; a status code is the number that says how it went, such as 200 for fine and 404 for not found.
  • Joel Spolsky, The Law of Leaky Abstractions (2002; the syllabus reading; ~12 min for the parts below). A programmer's essay about the contract an interface makes, and why no contract is perfect. He calls an interface's contract an abstraction. Read in three parts, and skip the rest, which needs programming you have not done yet:
  1. From the top down to the law in bold: "All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky." Non-trivial means not tiny. Broadway is in New York and Hollywood is in Los Angeles, on the other side of the country; steam coming out of your ears means angry.
  2. The last example in the list, the one about driving in the rain.
  3. The two paragraphs that begin The law of leaky abstractions means and And all this means. In 2002 he was warning about "code-generation tools": programs that write programs for you. The assistant you use is one. His line for it: "the abstractions save us time working, but they don't save us time learning." Wizzy means new and exciting.
