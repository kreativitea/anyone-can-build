Class 13 — Additional content

Assigned after class: do it before the midterm on Oct 30. The design doc takes about an hour if you finished most of it in class; on the Timeline route, writing your own product's takes about two. The midterm rehearsal from the second period has its own page.

Do: finish your design doc, and push it

Work in your product repository, with the files you copied from Timeline's docs/design-doc/ folder. The steps are in Timeline's README, under Write a design doc, and Timeline's own DESIGN.md is the finished example.

  1. DESIGN.md has all seven sections. If the period ran short, finish section 5 with maker.md and write section 6 yourself: three sentences, when ___, it should ___, and one edge case. Each sentence must be one a machine could settle.
  2. Section 7 is complete. Every objection from the checker has accept or reject, and one line of why in your own words. If you added sections after the checker ran, run checker.md again in a new session.
  3. AGENTS.md has one line: Read DESIGN.md before any change. If your repository has no AGENTS.md, create it with that line.
  4. Commit and push. Open your repository on GitHub and check that DESIGN.md is there.

Do: on the Timeline route, write your own product's design doc

If you used Timeline in class, your timeline-design/ folder is the practice. Now write the one for your own product, at the top level of your product repository, with the same steps. If you made a PR/FAQ deck, save it as a PDF in the folder and start with from-your-deck.md: your agent gives feedback, you choose which points to keep, and then it drafts sections 1 to 4, with every guess marked. If you did not, write sections 1 to 4 yourself. Then the maker, section 6, the checker, section 7, and the line in AGENTS.md, as above.

Read: Timeline's documents

  • Timeline's documents (~20 min; on the course site). The twenty-one documents from the tour at the start of class, all written for Timeline, in the order of the SDLC. Each one says who usually writes it, how it is reviewed, and what a small company does instead. Read the PRD's table Success, and how we will measure it, then follow the ticket TL-51 through its plan and its pull request to the bug report TL-58. Then read your own design doc once more, before you build anything from it.

Read: three documents you are responsible for

Every change to your product from now on gets a commit message and a pull request, and when something breaks, someone writes down what happened. Your agent will usually write them, but you are responsible for what they say: read each one, and fix it when it is unclear or wrong. What is easy for a person to read and understand is also easy for an agent to read and understand, and the next agent session starts from what is written. These three show what a good one looks like, from people who write them every day.

  • Chris Beams, How to Write a Git Commit Message (~8 min, 2014; free). An engineer's guide to the message that goes with every commit. Read The seven rules of a great Git commit message: a short subject line, a blank line, then a body that says what changed and why, not how. The parts about editors and the terminal are for later.
  • Google, Writing good CL descriptions (~5 min; free). Google's own rules for the description of a change; a CL is what Google calls a pull request. It says a description is "a public record of change". Read First Line, Body is Informative, and the bad and good examples. Skip the words you do not know in the examples.
  • John Allspaw, Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture (~8 min, 2012; free). The post at Etsy that made the blameless postmortem common practice, like INC-3 in Timeline's documents. Its main point: if people are punished for a mistake, they hide what happened, and the same failure comes back. "The action made sense to the person at the time they took it." You can skip the long list near the end.

Optional

  • Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google (~7 min for the part to read, 2020; free). A Google engineer on the design doc, the document you wrote in class. Read from Context and scope to the end of When not to write a design doc. His non-goals are your section 2, Not in this version. He is honest that "Writing design docs is overhead", and says when to skip one.
  • Jakob Nielsen, Paper Prototyping: Getting User Data Before You Code (~5 min, 2003; free). Why a design prototype comes before code: you can test a drawing of the screens with real people before anyone builds them. "Early beats late by so much that it outweighs the differences in prototype quality." You do not have to make one for your product. Read the first two sections; stop before the video.

Keep your data made up. No real person's name, words or data in your deck, your design doc or any prompt. Every example row is made up.
