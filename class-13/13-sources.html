Class 13 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 13's claims, so that any of them can be checked. Most of the class is the course's own material: the twenty-one documents on Timeline's documents were written for the course, about Timeline, and every name, number and incident in them is made up. Timeline's DESIGN.md and the files in its docs/design-doc/ folder were written for the course too. What comes from outside is the names of the documents, and where the field's terms for them come from.

Documents across the SDLC

  • Timeline's documents — the page opened from each stage slide, Idea to Analysis. The claim on Each phase has its own documents — the full set is the most rigor, and smaller teams skip some — is the instructor's, from working at large companies and at startups. No statistics are given, because none were found that would be honest. What a small team keeps is from the same experience.
  • Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, An insider look at Amazon's culture and processes (an excerpt from their book Working Backwards, 2021) — the PR/FAQ, "short for press release/frequently asked questions", the first Idea document on Idea, and the deck the own-idea route starts from. They were at Amazon when the PR/FAQ was introduced.
  • Eric Ries, Vanity Metrics vs. Actionable Metrics (19 May 2009) — the term vanity metric, said on Idea against the PRD's success table: "They might make you feel good, but they don't offer clear guidance for what to do." His example is the total number of hits to a website.
  • Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu, Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (the book's site, Cambridge University Press, 2020) — the standard account of guardrail metrics, the number that must not fall while a team chases its target, on Idea and again in the experiment doc on Analysis. The book is not free; the site has its contents and errata.
  • Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions (15 November 2011) — the architecture decision record on Design: "a short text file", one per decision, with its context and its consequences. He wrote it for the person who joins later and finds a decision with no reason beside it.
  • Personal Information Protection Commission, Act on the Protection of Personal Information — Japan's APPI, in the government's English translation, behind the data inventory on Design. Only the Japanese text has legal effect, as the page says.
  • GitHub Docs, Linking a pull request to an issue — a pull request whose description says Closes #10 closes issue 10 when it merges. That is the ticket and the pull request on Build.
  • Google, Site Reliability Engineering, chapter 15: Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure (2016; free to read) — "A postmortem is a written record of an incident, its impact, the actions taken to mitigate or resolve it, the root cause(s), and the follow-up actions". It calls blameless postmortems "a tenet of SRE culture". That is INC-3 on Prod. For the idea it cites John Allspaw's 2012 post at Etsy, Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture.

Studio: the design doc

  • Timeline — its DESIGN.md is the finished example on The design doc, on Timeline, and docs/design-doc/ holds design-template.md, from-your-deck.md, maker.md and checker.md, linked from Your own idea, or Timeline. Its README's Write a design doc has the steps.
  • Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google (2020) — a Google engineer's account of the design doc: "relatively informal documents" written before the coding starts, recording "key design decisions with emphasis on the trade-offs". His goals and non-goals are the course's section 2, Not in this version. The course's seven sections are its own shape, for a small team with no PRD.
  • AGENTS.md — the file an agent reads every time it starts, given one line pointing at DESIGN.md at the end of the studio.
  • Maker-checker and adversarial review, on Adversarial review (recall), are from Testing and Agentic engineering; their sources are on those classes' pages.
