Class 5 — Sources

Not homework. This is what Class 5 was built from — the papers, the data and the people behind the slides — so that any claim in the deck can be checked, and any of the fourteen examples followed further. Some of it is hard reading, and a few items are behind a paywall or on a site that only works over http; each line says so.

The opening and the close

  • Ada Lovelace, Note G, in L. F. Menabrea, Sketch of the Analytical Engine Invented by Charles Babbage (translated with her notes, Taylor's Scientific Memoirs 3, 1843) — the warning that opened and closed the class: first people overrate a new machine, then they undervalue it.
  • The New York Times, "New Navy Device Learns By Doing" (8 July 1958; subscription) — the 1958 overrating on The reality vs. the hype. Its exact words have not been checked against the newspaper, which is why the slide describes them instead of quoting them.
  • Seymour Papert, The Summer Vision Project (MIT AI Memo 100, July 1966) — the 1966 date in the close: object recognition, planned as the work of one summer for the lab's summer workers.
  • OpenAI, Better Language Models and Their Implications (14 February 2019) — the 2019 date in the close. OpenAI held back GPT-2 over "concerns about malicious applications of the technology" and released it in stages; "too dangerous to release" was the newspapers' phrase, not OpenAI's.

Rules

  • Joseph Weizenbaum, ELIZA — A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine (Communications of the ACM 9(1), 1966) — the program the room talked to, and its DOCTOR script.
  • Anthony Hay and Max Hay, ELIZA (a recreation, 2023–2025, CC0) — the page the room used, which runs the DOCTOR script as it was printed in 1966.
  • Joseph Weizenbaum, Computer Power and Human Reason (W. H. Freeman, 1976; borrowing it from the Internet Archive needs a free account) — what he wrote about ELIZA's effect on the people who used it, on What its author wrote about it.
  • Dan Jurafsky and James H. Martin, Speech and Language Processing (3rd edition draft) — the eight-line sketch of ELIZA's method behind How it chose every reply, from the January 2023 draft of chapter 15.
  • John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon, A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence (31 August 1955; http only) — where the field got its name, for a workshop in 1956.
  • Bruce G. Buchanan and Edward H. Shortliffe (eds.), Rule-Based Expert Systems: The MYCIN Experiments of the Stanford Heuristic Programming Project (Addison-Wesley, 1984; free on Shortliffe's own site) — the 1978 consultation and its WHY answer, in the Appendix, and MYCIN's explanations dated to 1973, in chapter 17.
  • Victor L. Yu and colleagues, An Evaluation of MYCIN's Advice (chapter 31 of Buchanan and Shortliffe's Rule-Based Expert Systems, edited from JAMA 242(12), 1979) — the blinded test on Who performed better?: 65% of MYCIN's prescriptions rated acceptable, against 55.5% for five specialists.

Learning from examples

  • Frank Rosenblatt, The perceptron: A probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain (Psychological Review 65(6), 1958; paywalled) — a machine that corrects itself when it is wrong.
  • Melanie Lefkowitz, Professor's perceptron paved the way for AI – 60 years too soon (Cornell Chronicle, 2019; a retelling) — the fifty tries on The reality vs. the hype.
  • Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert, Perceptrons (MIT Press, 1969; a book) — the limit of one straight line.
  • Adrien-Marie Legendre, Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des orbites des comètes (Paris, 1805; in French) — least squares, first published, in its appendix.
  • Carl Friedrich Gauss, Theoria motus corporum coelestium (Hamburg, 1809; in Latin, with an English translation from 1857) — his own least squares, and the orbit of Ceres.
  • Stephen M. Stigler, Gauss and the Invention of Least Squares (Annals of Statistics 9(3), 1981) — why It is called regression, and it is from 1805 says Gauss predicted Ceres correctly, and not that he used least squares to do it in 1801.
  • Cassie Kozyrkov, Making Friends with Machine Learning, Part 1 (2021) — the shape of the calorie sequence (guess, then the average, then one fact at a time), and the idea on Limitations of decision trees that methods differ mostly in the shape of line they are allowed to draw.
  • Bo Pang, Lillian Lee and Shivakumar Vaithyanathan, Thumbs up? Sentiment Classification using Machine Learning Techniques (EMNLP 2002) — two students' word lists, 58% and 64%, against counting, 81%.
  • Paul Graham, A Plan for Spam (2002) — the programmer on the classification card: one early rule, click, caught 79.7% of his spam, and counting words beat the rules he spent six months writing.
  • Stephanie Yee and Tony Chu, A Visual Introduction to Machine Learning, part 1 (R2D3, 2015) — the decision-tree slides borrow its shape: one question at a time, and the mistakes counted as each question goes in.
  • Leo Breiman, Jerome H. Friedman, Richard A. Olshen and Charles J. Stone, Classification and Regression Trees (1984; a book) — the decision tree.
  • Leo Breiman, Random Forests (Machine Learning, 2001), and Jerome H. Friedman, Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine (Annals of Statistics, 2001) — the hundreds of trees on the decision-tree card.
  • Tianqi Chen and Carlos Guestrin, XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System (KDD 2016) — boosted trees made fast and free, and the count of 2015's winning Kaggle solutions that used them.

Neural networks

  • David E. Rumelhart, Geoffrey E. Hinton and Ronald J. Williams, Learning representations by back-propagating errors (Nature 323, 1986; Hinton's own copy) — stack layers, and let every layer learn from the error at the end.
  • Seppo Linnainmaa, Algoritmin kumulatiivinen pyöristysvirhe yksittäisten pyöristysvirheiden Taylor-kehitelmänä (master's thesis, University of Helsinki, 1970; in Finnish), described in English by Andreas Griewank, Who Invented the Reverse Mode of Differentiation? (Documenta Mathematica, 2012) — the 1970 thesis on the neural-networks card, an earlier root of the method.
  • NobelPrize.org, The Nobel Prize in Physics 2024 — Hinton's prize, shared with John Hopfield, and in physics.
  • Daniel Smilkov and Shan Carter, TensorFlow Playground (Google, 2016) — the pattern that one straight line cannot split, on One straight line.
  • Kunihiko Fukushima, Neocognitron (Biological Cybernetics 36(4), 1980; paywalled; first published in Japanese in 1979) — the convolutional design, made at NHK's research laboratories. The Franklin Institute's 2021 award page tells his story in English.
  • Yann LeCun and colleagues, Backpropagation Applied to Handwritten Zip Code Recognition (Neural Computation 1(4), 1989; http only) — the design, trained end to end on 9,298 digits from the post office.
  • Yann LeCun, Léon Bottou, Yoshua Bengio and Patrick Haffner, Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition (Proceedings of the IEEE 86(11), 1998; http only) — its credit to Fukushima (p. 7), and the cheque reader (p. 1).
  • Adam Harley, 2D Visualization of a Convolutional Neural Network (2015) — the network you can draw a digit into, on A whole network, in the browser.
  • Michael I. Jordan, Serial Order: A Parallel Distributed Processing Approach (ICS Report 8604, UC San Diego, 1986) — a network with a memory carried from step to step.
  • Jeffrey L. Elman, Finding Structure in Time (Cognitive Science 14(2), 1990; paywalled) — the recurrent network, reading a sequence one word at a time.
  • Jia Deng and colleagues, ImageNet: A Large-Scale Hierarchical Image Database (CVPR 2009) — 3.2 million labelled photographs at the start, collected on purpose.
  • Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey E. Hinton, ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (NIPS 2012) — the network trained on two gaming graphics cards, drawn on How deep was it?
  • ImageNet, ILSVRC 2012 results — 15.3% against 26.2%, with a University of Tokyo team in second place.
  • Kaz Sato, How a Japanese cucumber farmer is using deep learning and TensorFlow (Google Cloud blog, 2016) — Makoto Koike's farm: over 95% in testing, about 70% in real use.

Transformers, and what comes next

  • Claude E. Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (Bell System Technical Journal 27(3), 1948) — the next-word text he made by hand from a book, on Who wrote this, and when? (p. 388).
  • Claude E. Shannon, Prediction and Entropy of Printed English (Bell System Technical Journal 30(1), 1951) — people guessing the next letter: the 1951 on the next-word card.
  • Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio, Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate (2014; ICLR 2015) — attention.
  • Andrej Karpathy, Stanford CS25: Introduction to Transformers (Stanford Online, January 2023) — Bahdanau's email about where attention came from, from about 18:30, and "delete everything, keep attention".
  • Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, Attention Is All You Need (NIPS 2017) — the transformer.
  • Jakob Uszkoreit, Transformer: A Novel Neural Network Architecture for Language Understanding (Google Research blog, 2017) — the sentence on Which word does "it" point at?: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", and "…too wide".
  • Aeree Cho and colleagues, Transformer Explainer (Georgia Tech, 2024) — used to check GPT-2's numbers.
  • Alec Radford, Karthik Narasimhan, Tim Salimans and Ilya Sutskever, Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training (OpenAI, 2018) — the first GPT, on the scale card.
  • Alec Radford and colleagues, Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (OpenAI, 2019) — GPT-2, which ran live in the room. Its weights are under OpenAI's Modified MIT License, which asks that its output be marked as GPT-2's.
  • Jared Kaplan and colleagues, Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (2020) — the fitted line on Ten times the data, the same step better.
  • Tom B. Brown and colleagues, Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (NeurIPS 2020) — GPT-3, and its published training compute.
  • Jaime Sevilla and Edu Roldán, Training compute of frontier AI models grows by 4-5x per year (Epoch AI, 2024) — the trend line on Ten thousand times the computing, in four years.
  • Paul Christiano and colleagues, Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences (NIPS 2017) — people choosing the better of two short clips of a machine at work, used as the training signal.
  • Long Ouyang and colleagues, Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (NeurIPS 2022) — InstructGPT. In its Figure 8, GPT-3 answers a French request for a story by writing more requests.
  • OpenAI, Introducing ChatGPT (30 November 2022) — the launch, as a free research preview.
  • Aaron Chatterji and colleagues, How People Use ChatGPT (NBER Working Paper 34255, 2025) — over a million registered users within five days, and about one adult in ten using it weekly by July 2025.
  • Joel Becker, Nate Rush, Elizabeth Barnes and David Rein, Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity (METR, 2025) — experienced developers 19% slower with AI, while believing they were 20% faster.
  • METR, We are Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design (2026) — the later study that leans the other way, which its own authors call very weak evidence.

Choosing a tier

  • The four tiers, and the rule try the lowest tier that solves the problem, come from the instructor's own work building software, not from a paper. Google's Rules of Machine Learning makes the same point in its Rule #1: "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning."

  • U.S. Department of Agriculture, FoodData Central, SR Legacy (public domain) — the 43 drinks behind How many calories? and every slide after it.
  • R. Quinlan, Auto MPG (UCI, 1993); Pınar Tüfekci and Heysem Kaya, Combined Cycle Power Plant (UCI, 2014); Athanasios Tsanas and Angeliki Xifara, Energy Efficiency (UCI, 2012); and the World Bank's World Development Indicators (all CC BY 4.0) — regressions on Two or three facts often predict well.

  • Hadi Fanaee-T, Bike Sharing (UCI, 2013, CC BY 4.0) — Washington DC's rental hours, for the vote and the network that learned live.
  • Allison Horst, Alison Hill and Kristen Gorman, palmerpenguins (2020, CC0), from data collected by Kristen B. Gorman, Tony D. Williams and William R. Fraser (PLOS ONE, 2014) — the penguins on Limitations of decision trees, and Horst's drawing of them.
  • Bo Pang and Lillian Lee, A Sentimental Education (ACL 2004), with its polarity dataset v2.0 — the film reviews your word lists were scored on.
  • Andrej Karpathy, tiny Shakespeare, in char-rnn (2015; the plays themselves are in the public domain) — the book the next-word counts on Pick the next word yourself came from.
  • Epoch AI, Data on AI Models (CC BY 4.0, read 18 September 2026) — every number on the compute chart, rounded.
