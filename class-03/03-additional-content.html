Class 3 — Additional content

Posted at the end of the Friday, together with Class 4's page — homework is batched at the end of the even class, never between the two periods of one afternoon. Short follow-up: do it before next week.

Do

  • Run the traceroute yourself. In class you saw one that started in a house in Japan and reached Seattle. Yours will be different, and that is the point — the route is a fact about where you are standing, not a fact about the internet. Open a terminal and run this on a Mac:
traceroute -I -a earthquake.usgs.gov

or this on Windows:

tracert earthquake.usgs.gov

Read the names, not just the numbers: they usually say where the machine is. Find the line where the time jumps. That jump is an ocean. Bring your list next week.

  • Sort a hand of cards and watch what you do. Deal yourself ten cards and put them in order, then notice how you did it. Most people pick up each card and slide it into the right place in their hand without ever deciding to — and that is insertion sort, which is in every textbook. You did not learn it here. You already had it.

Watch

  • Sabin Civil Engineering, How does the INTERNET work? (9 min, 2019). The trip your click makes, drawn plainly and slowly: a request, an address, a machine somewhere else, an answer. The same journey you watched in the browser's own panel, from further back. It goes deeper than we did — undersea cables, the physical wires — and that is the part worth staying for.
  • Timo Bingmann, 15 Sorting Algorithms in 6 Minutes (6 min, 2013). The race we ran in class, with fifteen methods instead of four. Each comparison is a note, so you hear the shape of an algorithm as well as see it. Watch how differently they behave: some crawl steadily, some do almost nothing and then finish in a rush.
  • Retro Report, From Y2K to 2038: Lessons Learned from the First Computer Crisis (9 min, 2018; the full transcript is on the same page). The Y2K story from class, told with news film from 1999 and by the people who did the work, including the man who ran the U.S. government's effort. For decades, programs stored the year as two digits to save space, so 2000 would read as 1900. They all ran correctly for years, because until the calendar reached 2000 no date ever touched that edge. People saw it coming, and teams went through old code, place by place, to fix it — which is why so little went wrong that night, and why some people now think it was never real. It ends on the next one, due in 2038. Japan had its own version in 2019, when the era changed from Heisei to Reiwa. A year before, the Guardian reported warnings of Japan's millennium bug, and a few days before the change, an ATM in a Lawson told a customer their money would arrive in May 1989.

Read

  • Simon Willison, Hallucinations in code are the least dangerous form of LLM mistakes (~6 min, 2025). Willison is a programmer who writes code with AI every day. When the AI invents something that does not exist, the code fails the moment you run it, so you see the mistake at once. The dangerous mistakes are the ones that run: no error, and the wrong answer. That is why we ran the code in class instead of only reading it — and why, as he puts it, proving that the code works is your job.
  • Simon Willison, Not all AI-assisted programming is vibe coding (~9 min, 2025). Vibe coding, the word from Karpathy's post in the first week, means building with AI without reading the code it writes. Willison says that if you read the code, tested it, and could explain how it works to someone else, you were not vibe coding: you were building software. That is the understanding check from class. He also says, in a part written for beginners, when vibe coding is fine: when a mistake could hurt nobody. And he warns about passwords and personal data.
  • Simon Willison, Here's how I use LLMs to help me write code (~5 min for two parts of a long post, 2025). Read the first part, Set reasonable expectations, and the part called You have to test what it writes! The rest is for later in the term.
  • Julia Evans, How I got better at debugging (one drawing, 2 min, 2016). Her first rule: the bug is happening for a logical reason. It is never magic, even when it makes no sense — the idea the class ended on. Look at the last panel too: what she thinks now when she finds a bug.
  • Julia Evans, Some ways to get better at debugging (~4 min, 2022). She sorts what you need to know to fix a bug into five kinds, and says what to do about each: the code itself, the system it runs on, your tools, your strategies, and experience. Four of the five were in class — the AI's code we read, the front and the back, the browser's own panel, and tracing and explaining it back. The fifth only comes with time.
  • U.S. General Accounting Office, Year 2000 Computing Challenge: Lessons Learned (the first ten pages of the full report, 2000). The U.S. government's own report on the Y2K problem from class, written after the year 2000 began, by the office that checks the government's work for Congress. In May 1997, 21 percent of the government's most important computer systems were ready for 2000, and by December 1999, 99.9 percent were. The report also lists what still broke: Medicare, the U.S. government's health insurance for older people, received more than 50,000 claims dated 1900 or 2099, and on 29 February 2000 a health-insurance system in Louisiana stopped for about ten hours because it did not recognize the date as valid.
  • BBC News, Gangnam Style music video 'broke' YouTube view limit (2 min, 2014). The row in class that asked will it run out of room?, with a real answer. YouTube's view counter had room for numbers up to 2,147,483,647, and in 2014 one video went past it. Its engineers had seen it coming and made the counter bigger. The same number is behind the 2038 problem at the end of the Retro Report film.
  • Julia Evans, HTTP status codes (one page, 1 min; the transcript is under it). The number that came back with every answer in class, on one page. Read the four families — 2xx, 3xx, 4xx, 5xx — then 200 and 404. Some of the others come later in the course.
  • xkcd, Dependency (one drawing, 30 seconds). Everything digital today, drawn as a tower of blocks — and near the bottom, holding it up, one small block that one person has been looking after, with no thanks, since 2003. Most of what your page runs on was written by people you will never meet. Like the earthquake feed: free to you, not free.

Read — the sorts, if you want them

You do not need to learn these, and nothing later in the course tests them. They are here because it is worth seeing that the thing you worked out in three minutes has a name, a history, and a measured cost — and because the Wikipedia article has animations of every one of them running.

Start here: Sorting algorithm — the overview, with the animations and the comparison table.

The three a room usually invents. All of them take about n² steps: double the cards and you roughly quadruple the work.

Selection sortfind the smallest, put it first, repeatfewest moves of any simple sort, which mattered when moving things was expensive
Insertion sorttake each card and slide it into placewhat people actually do with cards, and genuinely fast on a list that is nearly sorted already
Bubble sortkeep swapping neighbours until nothing swapsanalysed as early as 1956, and Wikipedia's own verdict is blunt: rarely used in practice, commonly found in teaching

The faster family. These take about n log n steps — for a thousand items, roughly ten thousand instead of a million.

Merge sortsplit in half, sort each half, mergealso the practical one for physical objects, because you can use both hands
Quicksortpick one card, put smaller left and larger right, repeatusually the quickest in practice, and can go badly on unlucky input
Heapsortbuild a structure that always knows its largest item, then empty itnever has a bad day; steady rather than fast

And the deliberately terrible ones. These exist because they are useful for thinking with, not for sorting anything.

Bogosortshuffle the whole deck, check whether it came out in order, repeatthe one we left out of the race. For its 22 cards, at a million shuffles a second, you would expect to wait more than thirty million years
Cycle sortwork out where each card belongs and put it straight therenot a joke — it is slow to decide but makes the theoretical minimum number of writes, which matters if writing is what costs you
Stooge sortsort the first two-thirds, then the last two-thirds, then the first two-thirds againcorrect, and almost unbelievably slow. Worth reading once for the shape of the argument that it works at all

Cycle sort is the interesting one of these three, and it makes a point the class race deliberately flattens: our race counted a comparison and a move as the same thing. Change that assumption — say moving a card is expensive and comparing is cheap — and the ranking changes. What counts as one step is itself a choice.

And the one your computer actually runs. Timsort — invented in 2002, built out of merge sort and insertion sort, and it is what sorts a list in Python, in Java and on Android. Insertion sort, what you already do with a hand of cards, is inside the thing running on your phone.

One name worth knowing. Among the people writing sorting algorithms around 1951 was Betty Holberton, who worked on ENIAC and UNIVAC. The first class of this course opened on Ada Lovelace. This field has been like that the whole time, and it is worth noticing who gets left out of the telling.
