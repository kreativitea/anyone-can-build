Class 3 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 3's claims, so that any of them can be checked: the history behind the code, the standards behind the web, and the people behind the earthquake feed. A few are books, and each line says so. Some lines say where a source is more exact than what was said in class.

Reading and testing code you didn't write

  • Josh Darnit, Exact Instructions Challenge - THIS is why my kids hate me. (YouTube, 2017) — the video the card sort called back to, when the room's own steps were followed exactly as written: a father makes a sandwich by doing what his children wrote, and nothing more.
  • Tim Bell, Ian H. Witten and Mike Fellows, Computer Science Unplugged (1998; the project is now at csunplugged.org) — computing taught with no computer, the tradition the card sort belongs to. Its activity Marching orders has children give instructions and follow them exactly, as the card sort did, and it is the earliest published classroom version of that we found. Its sorting activity says that card players often use insertion sort to put a hand in order.
  • David J. Malan, Lecture 0 (CS50x, Harvard University) — where this course found the order of the first beats: steps for a task written in plain words, called pseudocode, and then the parts of a program named from inside them. In CS50 the lecturer writes the steps; in this class the room did. Writing the steps before the code is much older than CS50, and where it began is not known.
  • Owen Astrachan, Bubble Sort: An Archaeological Algorithmic Analysis (SIGCSE 2003) — Which one did you write?: bubble sort was analysed in print in 1956, as "sorting by exchange", and the name bubble sort first appears in print in 1962, as far as Astrachan could find.
  • Mark Priestley, Routines of Substitution: John von Neumann's Work on Software Development, 1945–1948 (Springer, 2018; the free copy is the author's draft) — merge sort, the method that won the race, goes back to a program John von Neumann wrote in 1945, and insertion sort is in a lecture John Mauchly gave in 1946.
  • Donald E. Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching (Addison-Wesley, 1973; second edition 1998; a book) — the standard account of every sort in the race: where each came from, and how many steps each needs as the deck grows.
  • The step counts in the race, Same job. Different steps. Different speed, and on How many steps?, How do the steps grow? and How much room? come from the slides themselves: each one runs the methods on the cards and counts as it goes. The columns past 1,000 cards on And a million cards? are projected from those counts, and the slide says so.
  • Andrew Hunt and David Thomas, The Pragmatic Programmer (Addison-Wesley, 1999; a book), p. 95 — where the duck on Rubber duck debugging got its name. They call explaining your code out loud "rubber ducking", after a colleague who kept a small yellow rubber duck on his terminal while he worked. The page is in the publisher's free sample.
  • Brian W. Kernighan and Rob Pike, The Practice of Programming (Addison-Wesley, 1999; the debugging pages are free on Kernighan's site) — the same idea in the same year, with a teddy bear. One university computer centre made students explain a bug to a bear at the help desk before they could talk to a person.
  • Nicomachus of Gerasa, Introduction to Arithmetic (about 100 CE; translated by Martin Luther D'Ooge, 1926; public domain), Book I, chapter 13, p. 204 — the oldest surviving description of the Sieve of Eratosthenes, and it names him. Like the code on The same job — a different way, it works on the odd numbers only. Eratosthenes lived from about 275 to 194 BCE, so the idea is more than two thousand years old.
  • Robert William Hanks, answer to Fastest way to list all primes below N (Stack Overflow, 14 June 2010) — the nine lines of the sieve on The same job — a different way and One more input. He wrote it for an older version of Python, and Martijn Pieters changed it to today's in 2019 (CC BY-SA 4.0). The promise in its docstring, the primes below n, is Hanks's, but the two examples under it were added for this class. So the example on What goes in, what comes out, primes(10) → [2, 3, 5, 7], was written into the code by the class, not by its author. The answer at 1 comes from his code as it is.
  • Bill Sempf, On Testing (24 September 2014) — the list of orders on A tester walks into a bar, which he had posted the day before, reworking an older joke to make a point about testing the edges. Brenan Keller added the ending, the customer who asks where the bathroom is, on 30 November 2018.
  • U.S. General Accounting Office, Year 2000 Computing Crisis: An Assessment Guide (GAO/AIMD-10.1.14, September 1997) — the Y2K story, told in class: programs that kept the year as two digits could not tell 2000 from 1900.
  • U.S. General Accounting Office, Year 2000 Computing Challenge: Lessons Learned Can Be Applied to Other Management Challenges (GAO/AIMD-00-290, September 2000) — how the Y2K story ended. In May 1997, 21 percent of the U.S. government's most important computer systems were ready for 2000; by December 1999, 99.9 percent were.

Behind the scenes

  • U.S. Geological Survey, GeoJSON Summary Format — the shape of the answer read on And this is what came back and One of them, up close. What each name means is in the USGS's ComCat documentation: mag is the magnitude, place a description of where, time a count of milliseconds from the start of 1970, and the coordinates are longitude, latitude and depth.
  • U.S. Geological Survey, all_day.geojson — the address taken apart on Reading an address, and run on The other word on that line: every earthquake of the past day, updated every minute, free and with no key.
  • U.S. Geological Survey, Feed Life Cycle Policy — the facts behind Suppose they rename one word, which is a what-if. This feed has been version 1.0 since May 2013. A change that would break old code has to come as a new version, and a feed is removed only after at least 30 days' notice, sent to a mailing list for its users.
  • R. Fielding, M. Nottingham and J. Reschke (eds.), HTTP Semantics (RFC 9110, IETF, 2022), section 15 — the numbers on It came with a number, too. The first digit says what kind of answer it is: 2, success; 3, look somewhere else; 4, the request was wrong; 5, the server failed. The standard says one thing more carefully than the slide: a 404 means the server found nothing at that address, or will not say whether anything is there.
  • Larry Masinter, Hyper Text Coffee Pot Control Protocol (HTCPCP/1.0) (RFC 2324, 1 April 1998) — 418 I'm a teapot, on Two coffee pots. The slide has it the wrong way round: 418 is what a teapot answers when somebody tries to brew coffee with it. The RFC was an April Fools' joke, and it says itself that it is not a standard of any kind. It names the Cambridge coffee pot, and lists the camera's page among its references. Today's HTTP standard keeps 418 unused because the joke was used so widely.
  • Quentin Stafford-Fraser, The Trojan Room Coffee Pot: A (non-technical) biography (University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory, 1995) — the second pot on Two coffee pots. A camera was pointed at it in 1991, because some of the people who drank the coffee worked several flights of stairs away, and it took about three pictures a minute. It went on the web in November 1993, which made it, in its builders' words, the first webcam, and it took its last picture on 22 August 2001.
  • MDN Web Docs, Window: fetch() method and await — the two words on the line the half opened on, await fetch(url). fetch starts a request over the network, and await waits for the answer.
  • Chrome for Developers, Inspect network activity — the browser's own panel, used live on Whose computer is that? and Turning the network off.
  • FreeBSD, traceroute(8) (the manual page; macOS has the same program) — the command on Where is it, actually?: -I sends ICMP echo probes instead of the usual UDP ones, and -a looks up which network each machine belongs to. Van Jacobson wrote traceroute at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory in 1988, from an idea of Steve Deering's; its source code carries the date. The trace on the slide was run once, from a home in Japan, on 23 August 2026. A trace run somewhere else, or on another day, can take a different route.
  • The two network numbers on Where is it, actually? belong to two companies in the NTT group: AS2914 is NTT DATA's Global IP Network, and AS4713 is OCN, run by NTT DOCOMO BUSINESS.
  • Gavin P. Hayes and colleagues, National Earthquake Information Center strategic plan, 2019–23 (U.S. Geological Survey Circular 1457, 2019) — People, on It is free to you. It is not free: the center has been staffed around the clock since 2006, with at least two geophysicists always on duty.
  • U.S. Congress, the Earthquake Hazards Reduction Act of 1977, as it stands in 42 U.S.C. 7704(b)(3) — Rules: the USGS "shall report on significant domestic and international earthquakes", words added to the law in 2018.
  • U.S. House Committee on Appropriations, Explanatory statement regarding H.R. 6938 (Congressional Record, 8 January 2026, p. H476; the bill became law on 23 January 2026) — Money: $94,901,000 for earthquake hazards in fiscal year 2026.
  • U.S. Department of the Interior, Budget Justifications and Performance Information, Fiscal Year 2027: U.S. Geological Survey (2026), pp. 35–37 — the same program over the years: $92.65 million in 2025, $94.90 million in 2026, and a request for $62.65 million in 2027, about a third less. The amount is decided again every year.
