Do: follow your data from your app to the database, and back

In these four steps you watch one piece of data make the whole trip: from your app, to your Supabase table, and back to your screen. Start from what you finished in class. Each step needs the step before it, so go in this order. When all four work, commit and push.

First, check that Claude can reach Supabase. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a standard way for Claude to use another service's tools for you. In class you connected Supabase's MCP server. In Claude Code, type /mcp: the list should show supabase. If it does not, choose supabase, then Authenticate, and sign in.

  1. Show a row from your table. Open your Supabase dashboard, go to Table Editor, and open your table. If it has no rows, add one there by hand, with made-up data. Then open your app: it should show that row. If it does not, ask Claude: my app should show the rows in my Supabase table, but it shows nothing. Why? Reload the page. The row is still there.
  2. Save one thing from your app. Open your app and use it the way a user would. For example, if your app lets people write reviews, write a review and save it. If your app has sign-in, sign in first. Reload the page. It is still there. Then go back to Table Editor in the dashboard and find the row you just saved. If it is not in the table, your app did not save it.
  3. Turn on row-level security, and write one policy. Row-level security is a lock on your table. When it is on, nobody can read or change a row unless a policy allows it. A policy is one rule for one table, for example: anyone may read all rows, and anyone may add a row.
  • Ask Claude to turn on row-level security for your table.
  • Reload your app. It shows no rows. This is correct. It means the lock works. Do not turn it off again.
  • Ask Claude to write one policy for your table, and read it before you approve it.
  • Reload your app. Your rows are back.
  • Write the policy in one sentence, in your own words: who may do what, to which rows. This sentence goes on your integration card (below).
  1. Run the round-trip test. A round-trip test saves a row, reads it back, checks that it is the same, then deletes it, so that the test can run again tomorrow. It checks one thing: your app and your database really work together. Ask Claude: write a round-trip test for my Supabase table, and tell me the command to run it. Read the test. Find the line that deletes the row. Run it: type the command Claude gives you in your terminal, and press Enter. The test passes, or it fails and you can explain why.

Do: check your keys before you commit

Your repository is public. Anyone on the internet can read anything you commit. So before every commit, look at the files you are about to commit.

Supabase gave you three values. Two of them go in your app, and one never does:

  • The Project URL and the Publishable key go in your app, in one config file. A config file is the one file that holds your app's settings. You made it in class. If you do not remember which file it is, ask Claude: which file has my Supabase Project URL? This file is committed, and that is safe: the Publishable key is made to be seen by anyone who opens your page. Row-level security is what protects your data, not this key.
  • The Secret key never goes anywhere. Do not copy it into your code, into any file, or into a chat with Claude. Nothing you build in this course needs it.

Then open your .gitignore file. It lists the files that git must never commit. If you have a .env file, or any other file with a password in it, its name must be in .gitignore.

Do: your integration card, in your tracker

Your tracker is your private repository anyone-can-build-tracker, where your journal is. Your integration card is six lines about how you connected Supabase to your app. You had a printed card in class. Write the six lines in your tracker, under the heading Integration card, in your own words:

  1. What I connected, and the part I decided not to build myself. One sentence each.
  2. The two keys: which one is in my app and in which file, and which one I never copied.
  3. What data leaves my app now: what a person types, where it goes, and who can read it.
  4. Row-level security: on or off, and what my policy says, in words.
  5. What my policy will say once people can sign in.
  6. My round-trip test: what it checks, and what happened the first time I ran it.

I read lines 3 and 4 most carefully. Class 18's page adds a few more lines to this same card.

Do: sign-in, only if your app needs it

Ask this question about your app: must two different users not see each other's rows? For example, a private diary must hide each person's entries from other people. A shared list of restaurants does not.

  • If the answer is no, skip this step.
  • If the answer is yes, your app needs sign-in, so that Supabase knows who each user is. Ask Claude to add Supabase Auth, which is Supabase's sign-in. Then ask for a second policy: each user may read only their own rows. Test it with two made-up accounts: sign in as one, then as the other, and check that each one sees only its own rows.

Do: resume your Supabase project, if it is paused

A free Supabase project that nobody uses for seven days is paused. Paused does not mean deleted: your data and your settings are kept. It means the project is not running, so your app cannot read or save anything. Supabase does not start it again by itself.

The next class is on Friday, Nov 13, seven days after the last one. On Thursday, Nov 12, open your Supabase dashboard. If it says paused, click Resume project and confirm. It is not instant, so do not leave it until class starts. Better still, use your app a few times during the week. Then the project is never paused.

Use only fake data. No real person's name, address or phone number in your table. Your data is now on another company's servers, and the free plan keeps no backup copy of it.
