Class 17 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 17's claims, so that any of them can be checked. Some of the class is the course's own material: Gaidai Eats, its move to Supabase and the times measured on it, the five steps for buying a service, the deciding question and the seven tradeoffs were made for the course. What comes from outside is the Iron Ring and the Québec Bridge, the facts about names and email addresses, one security incident from 2025, and how Supabase behaves.

What is engineering, and the messy real world

  • The Corporation of the Seven Wardens, History — the ring on The Iron Ring. Professor H. E. T. Haultain proposed it in 1922. Rudyard Kipling wrote the texts of the ceremony, and "The first ceremony took place on April 25, 1925, in Montreal." The ring is worn on "the little finger of the working hand". That it touches the paper when you sign is the usual explanation for the finger; this page does not give it.
  • Government of Canada, The history of the Québec Bridge (2019) — the bridge in the same story. On 29 August 1907 the south section fell and 76 workers died, "including 33 Mohawk workers from the Kahnawake community." On 11 September 1916 it fell again and 13 more died. Other sources give 75 or 77 for 1907.
  • Henry Petroski, To Forgive Design: Understanding Failure (Harvard University Press, 2012) — a book, not free to read. It is the source usually cited for the point made on The Iron Ring: the story that the first rings were made from the bridge's steel is not true. The Wardens' own page says only that the first rings were iron.
  • Gaidai Eats, the midterm repository, which is private because it will be the midterm again — the counts on Your midterm: search places. They were counted on its own snapshot of places, data/places.json. That 211 of the 1,189 places have an English name, on The messy real world, is from the same file.
  • OpenStreetMap, Copyright and License — where the places came from. Real people wrote them, mostly in Japanese. The data is "licensed under the Open Data Commons Open Database License".
  • Patrick McKenzie, Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names (June 2010) — the idea behind A sign-up form: first name, last name. His list starts with "People have exactly one canonical full name." The three people on the slide were chosen for the course, not taken from his list, so the slide says after him.
  • J. Klensin, RFC 3696: Application Techniques for Checking and Transformation of Names (February 2004), section 3 — the four addresses on Email addresses. It lists them with others that "are valid and are seen fairly regularly". Written for programmers.
  • Phil Haack, I Knew How To Validate An Email Address Until I Read The RFC (21 August 2007) — the post that made that list famous, also on Email addresses. He admits he "had never actually read (or even skimmed) the RFC". His list also has addresses with a backslash; the slide leaves them out, because people disagree about whether they are valid.

Build or buy

  • The three bridge photographs on "Build a bridge," again are the ones from the first Friday. Their credits are on Class 1's Sources page, under The pictures on the slides.
  • The deciding question, Is this the reason your product exists?, the seven tradeoffs on What buying costs, and the five steps on Five steps, every time you buy one are the course's own way of saying it. They are not quoted from a book.
  • The numbers on What moving the database cost are the course's own measurement, made on 5 November 2026 on a copy of Gaidai Eats that is not public. From Japan, one query to a Supabase project in Sydney took 189 ms. Loading the 1,189 places one at a time ran for about 20 minutes and then failed; loading them in one request took 3 seconds. No source outside the course is claimed for them.
  • Render, Deploy for Free — the last row of Two stacks, one database: a free web service "spins down" after 15 minutes with no traffic coming in, and starting again "takes about one minute". The site on Live: Gaidai Eats, on the internet is gaidai-eats-fmvs.onrender.com.
  • Supabase, Sending emails — why Which sign-in? says you need an email service either way: Supabase's own sender "is not meant for production use". django-allauth is the library named in the same table for Google sign-in on a Django site.

Supabase, step by step

  • Anthropic, Introducing the Model Context Protocol (25 November 2024) — where MCP comes from, on MCP, Model Context Protocol. The protocol's own site, modelcontextprotocol.io, calls it "an open-source standard for connecting AI applications to external systems."
  • Supabase, Model context protocol (MCP) — the server added at Station 1, https://mcp.supabase.com/mcp, and the four tool names on MCP, Model Context Protocol. Sign-in is in the browser. The warning read each call before you approve it is Supabase's own: "review each tool call before you run it."
  • Supabase, Understanding API keys — A URL, a key, and a key you never copy and A page in a browser cannot keep a secret. Publishable keys start with sb_publishable_ and are "Safe to expose online". Of a secret key it says: "Never put one in a browser, a shipped application, or source control." A publishable key can "only reach what Row Level Security allows."
  • Matt Palmer, Statement on CVE-2025-48757 (29 May 2025) — This happened, 2025. He found the problem on 20 March 2025 with his colleague Kody Low: 170 of the 1,645 Lovable projects they checked had tables anyone could read. The emails he quotes list names, emails, phone numbers, payment details and API keys. The problem was "not an exposed public API key" but "the absent RLS configuration". Lovable disputes the CVE.
  • Supabase, Row Level Security — Row-level security, and a policy, Live: Gaidai Eats, with only the public key and Station 6. With row-level security on and no policy, "no data is accessible through the API" with a publishable key. A policy is like "adding a WHERE clause to every query", and the page has an example that lets anyone read the rows.
  • Supabase, Database Backups — the last box on What data leaves your app. Daily backups are for "Pro, Team, and Enterprise Plan projects"; a free project should export its own data.
  • Supabase, Project Pausing — Before you close the laptop. Free projects "that show low activity over a 7-day period" are paused, and you bring one back yourself, with Resume project in the dashboard.
  • Personal Information Protection Commission, Act on the Protection of Personal Information — Japan's APPI, behind the box about personal information on What data leaves your app.
