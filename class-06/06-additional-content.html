Class 6 — Additional content

Assigned after class, on top of the syllabus outside-work. Short follow-up (basically light homework): do it before next week.

Watch

  • R2D3, A Visual Introduction to Machine Learning — Stephanie Yee and Tony Chu. Scroll it slowly; it takes about ten minutes and there is nothing to read closely. It sorts homes into two groups, San Francisco or New York, from measurements of the home, and nobody wrote a rule for it — the same idea you built, on somebody else's data. Two things to watch for. The moment a split is chosen because it separates the two groups well: that is the machine working out a rule nobody wrote. And, further down, the moment it checks the model on homes it was not trained on, and does worse. You already know that one. You watched it happen on your partner's hands.

Read

  • Addy Osmani, Shubham Saboo and Sokratis Kartakis (Google / Kaggle), "The New SDLC With Vibe Coding" (June 2026, ~50 pages (confirm)). You do not have to read all of it. Read the first two sections and stop when it starts talking about large engineering teams. This is engineers writing down, this year, the same loop you have been living since the first Friday of this course: describe what you want, let the machine write it, then check it. Watch for two things. First: where they say the checking step is the job — that is what you did all Friday afternoon, trying your model on hands it had never seen, written down by people who do it for a living. Second: the line they draw between asking for code and accepting whatever comes back, and directing the machine and judging the result. They have a name for the difference. Keep it in mind for next week, when you start building your own product.
  • (confirm) Kaggle may ask you to sign in to open the PDF. If it does and you would rather not make an account, read Osmani's own free summary instead: The New Software Lifecycle. It is shorter and it keeps the figures.
  • Jared Wilber and Brent Werness, Train, Test, and Validation Sets (~10 min, Amazon's MLU-Explain; short text, and pictures you move). Cats and dogs sorted by weight and fluffiness: drag the animals across the line and watch the model's scores change. It explains why some examples must be kept back — the check set from Friday, which it calls the test set — and it ends on the rule the room kept when the two testers were chosen before anyone trained: never look at the test set before you choose your model. It also uses a third set, validation, that we did not; think of it as a second check set you are allowed to use while you are still improving the model.

Keep in mind

Five short pieces for the questions Friday ended on: accurate, measured on whom?, and what happens when the picture is not the one it was trained on? Keep them in mind whenever anyone — you included — says how accurate a model is.

  • Joy Buolamwini, Gender Shades (5 min video, 2018; captions written by people). She tested three large companies' systems for reading faces — each guessed whether a face was a man's or a woman's — on a set of faces she put together herself, as the room chose its own check set. Overall, each looked accurate. Split by group, the errors reached 34.7% for darker-skinned women and at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men — and the faces normally used to test such systems were mostly lighter-skinned. The question to keep: accurate on whom?
  • Google PAIR, From Confidently Incorrect Models to Humble Ensembles (~10 min, 2023; the first two sections — stop at Combining Models Reduces Overconfidence). You draw, and a model trained on handwritten digits — the MNIST digits from What is AI? — says which digit it sees. Draw something that is not a digit at all, and it still names one, and it is sure. That is the empty frame on Friday: a model can only answer with the labels it was given, so the hundred points always land somewhere, unless it has a None to put them in.
  • Stephanie Yee and Tony Chu, A Visual Introduction to Machine Learning, Part 2 (~10 min, R2D3, 2018; stop at The Bias-Variance Tradeoff). The second part of R2D3's explainer, with the same San Francisco and New York homes as the first. Grow the tree until every group is pure, and one group ends up built from just two homes — so every test home that lands in it is called wrong. A model that is perfect on its own examples has learned those examples, not the world; the check set is how you find out. English only, where part 1 has twelve languages.
  • Google Research, Introducing the Inclusive Images Competition (1 min: look at the first picture, 2018). A model trained on a large public collection of photographs labels three weddings with white dresses ceremony, wedding, bride — and a couple in wedding clothes from outside the West only person, people. It did not learn wedding; it learned the weddings in its photographs. Friday's it did not learn rock, it learned your rock, in a much larger model.
  • BR Data (Elisa Harlan and Oliver Schnuck), Objective or Biased (~12 min, 2021; stop after A study in sepia). Journalists at Bayerischer Rundfunk, a German public broadcaster, sent job-interview videos to an AI that scores people's personality, and changed one thing at a time: glasses, a headscarf, a bookshelf behind the person, the light. Each time the scores moved, though the person and the answers were the same. The company said its system was 90 percent accurate. It is Friday's Add noise, on a product sold to help companies choose whom to hire.

Bring

  • Two or three real problems. Next week you choose one, and you carry it for the rest of the term — it becomes the thing you build, ship, and defend in December. So bring three, so you have a choice.
  • A real problem is:
  • Someone's actual problem. Yours, your roommate's, your club's, your part-time job's. Someone you can go and ask questions of.
  • Repeating. It happened last month and it will happen again next month. Not a one-time annoyance.
  • Describable in two sentences, without using the word "app." "My circle books practice rooms in a LINE group and two people always book the same slot" is a problem. "A booking app" is not — that is already an answer, and it skips the part where you find out what is actually wrong.
  • Write each one down like this, three lines each:
  • Who has this problem (a specific person or group, not "students").
  • What goes wrong, and how often.
  • What they do now instead — the messy workaround. There is always one.
  • It does not need to be a software problem, and it does not need to be big. Small and real beats big and imaginary every time.
