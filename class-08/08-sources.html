Class 8 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 8's claims, so that any of them can be checked. The first half of the class is case studies of real things, so most of this page is those things themselves: a lesson, a keynote, three pitches and two press releases. Where a source could not be read directly, the entry says how it was read instead.

Case study 0: eight teapots

  • Code.org, CS Discoveries (2020), Unit 4 Lesson 1, Analysis of Design, and its activity guide Determine the User (curriculum CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; third-party artwork excepted) — the eight teapots, the people to match them to, and I like · I wish · What if, on the three teapot slides. Code.org's current version of this lesson matches shoes to people; the teapots are in the 2020 version, which is the one linked.
  • Jacques Carelman, Catalogue of Unfindable Objects — teapot G, with the spout on the same side as the handle. The guide's picture carries his credit inside it, in Italian.

Case study 1: the iPhone

  • Steve Jobs, the Macworld keynote, San Francisco, 9 January 2007, in the Internet Archive's copy (1 h 19 min; the slide's times, 0:00 to 3:40, are for this copy) and with a transcript kept by a fan archive — the problem said before any demonstration, the four phones and "not so smart and not so easy to use", on the three iPhone slides. Apple does not host the keynote; the transcript's times belong to an upload that no longer plays.
  • Apple, Apple Reinvents the Phone with iPhone (9 January 2007) — the iPhone went on sale in June, on What the audience did not see, and the first card of Two real press releases.
  • Fred Vogelstein, And Then Steve Said, 'Let There Be an iPhone' (New York Times Magazine, October 2013), from his book Dogfight (2013) — the golden path, several phones on stage, five bars always and about a hundred rehearsals, on What the audience did not see. Behind a subscription, so it was not read directly: every detail was checked against long quotations of it in other publications.
  • Daniel Eran Dilger, Android docs reveal before iPhone, Google's plan was a Java button phone (AppleInsider, 14 April 2014), with John Gruber's No One Said Following Is Easy (Daring Fireball, April 2014) — Android's own plans, entered as evidence in Apple v. Samsung: in 2006, "touch screens will not be supported", on Meanwhile, at Google.
  • Fred Vogelstein, Dogfight (2013) — Chris DeSalvo's "We're going to have to start over" and Vogelstein's telling of Andy Rubin's words, on the same slide. Read through quotations of the book, not the book itself.
  • OSnews, Sooner prototype dropped "well before the iPhone announcement" (December 2013) — Dianne Hackborn remembers the keyboard phone being dropped before the iPhone, on the same slide. Her original post is gone; this page quotes it.

Case study 2: three pitches

  • Airbnb's 2008 pitch deck, in a copy on SlideShare uploaded in 2015 by somebody outside the company — the problem slide and the counts on Which stage was each one at? and What all three said first, and the revenue projection on And every one of them was wrong somewhere. No copy published by Airbnb itself was found.
  • Drew Houston, My YC app: Dropbox - Throw away your USB drive (Hacker News, 4 April 2007), and the replies it drew — the second pitch, and the replies on And every one of them was wrong somewhere and The questions get asked anyway.
  • Reid Hoffman, LinkedIn's Series B Pitch to Greylock (2013), the 2004 deck with his own notes, one of which calls a slide "mostly a mistake slide"; also on SlideShare — the third pitch, on the same three slides.

Your problem, and its press release

  • Werner Vogels, Working Backwards (2006) — Amazon writes the press release and the frequently asked questions before it builds, on Half of this is for an investor. Vogels was Amazon's chief technology officer, and this is the earliest public account of the method found.
  • Taylah Hasaballah and Anthony Bertuca (Google Maps), Grab a seat and be on time with new transit updates on Google Maps (27 June 2019) — a press release for one new feature that starts with the problem, on Two real press releases.
