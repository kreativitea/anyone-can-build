Class 8 — Additional content

Assigned after class, on top of the syllabus outside-work: do it before next week. Most of it is the Do list: your page, one interview, and your tracker entry. The reading is short, and each piece helps with one of those three.

Do

  • Type your PR/FAQ into your tracker. Copy it across from your copy of the studio template into your anyone-can-build-tracker repository: the five lines of the press release, the three FAQ answers, and the three columns, with one sentence beside everything in Not this product that says why not. Then commit, push, and open the repository on GitHub to check that it is there. So you are not guessing what I read for: line four is a real person's words, and Not this product has something in it that cost you. The syllabus calls this page your product one-pager; it is the same page.
  • Write this week's tracker entry, in the same five fields as every week. Where I am on the loop: Idea. What I decided: the problem you are carrying, and for whom. Why, and what I did instead: why this one, and the two you crossed out. What I could not explain: the honest one, the part of your page you could not answer when your partner asked. Did it work?: not yet, so write the sentence that will one day tell you. In November, when analytics go into your product, that sentence is what becomes a number.
  • Interview one person who is not in this class, and bring back one sentence they actually said. The next section says what counts. If you cannot find anybody to ask, that is information, not laziness: tell me before Friday.
  • If you are going to change your problem, change it this week. This is the cheapest week to change it, and it is not the last one. A change costs a new card, a new interview and a rewritten page, on your own time, and none of it is a mark against you. Write the change in your tracker, with the reason. A change you can explain is a decision.

What counts as an interview

  • Who. One real person who is not in this class, and who might have your problem: a roommate, a friend, somebody in your club, somebody at your part-time job, somebody in your family.
  • The three questions from Friday, in this order. When did this last happen to you? · What did you do? · Have you tried to fix it before? They ask about something that already happened, so the answers are about what the person did, not about what they imagine they would do.
  • Do not describe your idea. The moment you describe the product, people become polite, and a polite answer tells you nothing. If they ask what you are building, say you will tell them afterwards.
  • Write their words, not your summary, in whichever language the two of you share. The sentence you bring back is theirs, exactly as they said it. You can translate it afterwards.
  • No names on the page. Write a second-year student who commutes, not a person's name, because your tracker is a repository on GitHub. Tell the person what the interview is for, and ask before you write down anything they say.
  • What does not count: a classmate; an AI assistant; a survey or a poll; asking would you use this?; and your own guess of what they would say. "This is not a problem for me" counts, and it is a useful answer: write it down.

The finished sheet from Friday

The sheet that was on the screen for thirty seconds before you wrote your own, here to keep beside you while you type. It was written for teaching: the laundry page is an example, not a real product, and the quote is an example too. Your page comes from your card and your interview, not from this.

  • One line. From today, students in Building 3 can see which laundry machines are free from their phone.
  • The problem. Students in Building 3 · a basket carried down four floors to find a busy machine · most wash days · they go early, or they wait.
  • What changes. They check from their room, and go down once.
  • One sentence from a real person. "I went down three times on Sunday and every machine was taken."
  • How to start. Open the link. The machines are on the screen.
  • Who is this not for? Students in other buildings, in version one.
  • What is not in the first version? Reserving a machine.
  • What would make me stop? Nobody opens the page for two weeks.
  • In version one: one page: free or busy. Later: a message when a machine becomes free. Not this product: reserving, because a reserved machine stands empty while somebody walks down.

Watch

  • Therese Fessenden (Nielsen Norman Group), Five User Interview Mistakes to Avoid (in 5 Minutes) (6 min video, 2022; English captions written by people). Watch it before your interview. Nielsen Norman Group trains companies to do user research, and this is their short video on what goes wrong in an interview. Watch for the difference between a leading question and a probing one. A leading question suggests its own answer, as would you use an app that…? does. A probing question asks for more of what they already said, as what did you do then? does. None of Friday's three questions leads: each one asks about something that already happened. One difference from your interview: their written list of five mistakes warns against taking notes while you talk, because a researcher can have a second person take them. You are on your own, so write down only the sentences you will use, in the person's own words.

Read

  • GOV.UK Service Manual, Using in-depth interviews (~3 min: read Do the interview and After the interview). The UK government's guide for the teams that build its public services, written in plain English on purpose. Two lines are worth carrying into your interview: "focus on stories and real examples", and "try to stay quiet - the more you talk, the less your participant will talk". It also says an interview can happen almost anywhere, including by phone or video call.
  • Drew Houston and the people who replied, My YC app: Dropbox - Throw away your USB drive (Hacker News, April 2007; ~5 min for the three replies below). The second of the three pitches from Friday, with the replies it drew. Read three of them, each at its own link:
  • The top reply, which says you can already build this yourself, and Houston's answer, which answers with specifics rather than defending himself. This is the FAQ arriving whether you wrote it or not.
  • A reply that names the real competition: not USB drives, but email, which is what people already did with their files. It is what they do now, from the four parts of a problem.
  • Houston's answer to "was Dropbox your first idea?". It was not: he had six or seven ideas at once, and spent the time talking to people who might use them rather than writing code. That is the idea loop, told by somebody who ran it.
  • Werner Vogels, Working Backwards (~3 min, 2006; stop at Define the customer experience). Amazon's chief technology officer on the method the PR/FAQ comes from: the press release and the questions are written first, before anything is built. Watch for his reason: "Writing a press release up front clarifies how the world will see the product". The third step on his list, a picture of every screen, is next Friday's class, which is why you stop there.

Try

  • The press release a product deserved. Pick a product you already use every week, and write the five lines of the press release it should have had when it launched: one line in the words of the person who has the problem, the problem, what changes, one sentence from a real person, and how to start. It is the same move as the line you wrote for your own page from week one, on something bigger. It is practice, and nobody reads it but you.

Keep in mind

  • Christensen Institute, Jobs to Be Done (1 min: read the short section Morning milkshakes). The institute Clayton Christensen founded, on the idea he is known for. A fast-food restaurant found that people bought a milkshake in the morning to stay full and to make their drive more interesting, and that its competition "wasn't other fast food milkshakes" but bananas and bagels. It is the point of The shape all three were written in: whatever people do now is the competition, even when it is a different kind of thing.
  • Alex Hern, Netflix's biggest competitor? Sleep (~3 min, The Guardian, 2017; stop when it reaches Square, the payments company). Asked about Netflix's competitors, its chief executive, Reed Hastings, said he was not worried about Amazon or HBO: when a show keeps you watching late at night, "We're competing with sleep, on the margin." The article adds two more: Uber's real rival, it says, is the self-driving car, and Facebook's are video games and TV. Read it for the slide The shape all three were written in: what people do now is the competition, and for a show you watch at night, what people do now is go to sleep.
