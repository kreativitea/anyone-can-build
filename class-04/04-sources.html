Class 4 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 4's claims, so that any of them can be checked. Most of this class came from practice — the game played across the table, and the instructor's own work building products — so the list is short: where two of its words come from, an older name for its main idea, and the pictures.

Name the loop

  • The five stages on It has a name — Idea, Design, Build, Prod and Analysis — and the plain words under each are this course's own way of drawing the loop, not a standard's.
  • Amazon Web Services, What is SDLC? — the industry's plain description of the software development lifecycle, and the reading on Before next Friday. It counts six stages where the course counts five: plan, design, implement, test, deploy and maintain.
  • The name MVP, minimum viable product, on It has a name, is usually credited to Frank Robinson of the product-development firm SyncDev, who dates his first use of it to 2001 (SyncDev's page, as archived in 2016). Eric Ries made it widely known from 2009, in Minimum Viable Product: a guide. His definition is about learning — the version that teaches you the most about your customers for the least effort — which is close to the ring's the smallest version that actually works, but not the same.
  • Barry W. Boehm, Guidelines for Verifying and Validating Software Requirements and Design Specifications (TRW, 1979; the University of Southern California's copy, as archived) — an older name for the split on "It runs" is not "it worked". The slide's two questions are the course's own words; Boehm asked the same two in 1979, as "Am I building the product right?" (verification) and "Am I building the right product?" (validation). Testing is one way to answer either one.
  • The success line on You can only answer it if you said it first — one sentence, written before you build, that can be answered yes or no — is the course's own.

Walk the loop

  • The Strong National Museum of Play, Battleship (National Toy Hall of Fame, 2025) — the game on You cannot see their board. It was a pencil-and-paper game long before Milton Bradley's plastic edition of 1967, and the museum describes the lid that hides each player's ships from the other.
  • The game in this class — one of you builds, the other uses, and only words cross the table — is the course's own, and so is the tracker.
  • What makes a good product good was spoken, with no slide. The instructor worked through a few real products, Google Maps among them, from his own experience of building products.
