Class 4 — Additional content

Assigned after class, on top of the syllabus outside-work. Short follow-up (basically light homework): do it before next week.

Watch

  • Tom Wujec, Build a tower, build a team (~7 min; the transcript is on the same page). The marshmallow challenge: small teams get eighteen minutes to build the tallest tower they can from spaghetti, tape and string, with one marshmallow on top. Business-school graduates plan it once and build it once, and their tower falls when the marshmallow goes on at the very end. Kindergarten children build, test, and build again — and they do better. That is the loop from today, and it is why your page went live empty in the first five minutes.
  • Steve Krug, Usability Test Demo (~24 min). A real, unedited session: one person is given a website and a task, and the facilitator sits next to them and mostly stays quiet. This is the Analysis stage done properly. Today you could not see your partner's screen, so their words were all you had; he can see everything, and he still says almost nothing. Watch for the two hard parts: how he asks the person to say what they are thinking, and how long he lets them struggle before he says anything. Count the number of times you would have jumped in and explained.

Read

  • AWS, "What is SDLC?" (~10 min). The reading from the Before next Friday slide: the industry's plain description of the loop we named today, so it should look familiar. AWS counts six stages where we count five, and names some of them differently — as you read, find where each of our five sits in their list.
  • Henrik Kniberg, Making sense of MVP (~15 min, 2016; the first drawing carries the point). On the loop today, Build was also written MVP: minimum viable product. His first drawing shows two ways to give someone a car. One team builds the car in parts, and the person can use nothing until the very end. The other gives them a skateboard first, then a scooter, a bicycle, a motorbike, and at last a car — something they can use, and say something about, at every step. In the game, every turn of Play left your partner a page they could open and try: a skateboard, not a wheel. He suggests better names for it, such as the earliest usable product.
  • Akio Shiibashi (JR East), Introduction and Future Development of Suica (~5 min: read First Field Trial and Third Field Trial). JR East's own account of the Suica gate you walk through every day, by the senior manager of its Suica project. In the first trial with real passengers, in 1994, people were stopped at the new gates twenty times as often as with the old magnetic tickets. Some of the causes were in the machine, and one was in the people: every passenger held the card in a different place. In the third trial the place to touch was clearly marked, and the method became "Touch and Go" — and the errors fell at once to the level of the old tickets. The gate's designer, Shunji Yamanaka, tells the same story from the design side on his blog (in Japanese; your browser's translate button handles it): careful watching of a few people mattered more than testing many, and he tilted the reader 13.5 degrees so that a hand rests on it for a moment. That is the difference between "it runs" and "it worked".
  • Jakob Nielsen, "First Rule of Usability? Don't Listen to Users" (~5 min). Short and blunt: what people say they will do is a poor guide, and what they actually do in front of you is a good one. Read it against today's game. The success line was settled by what the product person did: they tried the page, and it worked or it did not. The one thing they would change is what they said. This piece explains why the first is worth more than the second.
  • Wikipedia, Tree swing cartoon (one picture, 1 min). Four drawings of one swing on a tree: what marketing suggested, what was manufactured, how maintenance put it up, and — last — what the customer wanted, which was a tyre on a rope. It is the second question from Last call: what came back different from what you meant? Nobody knows who drew it first; versions have been passed around since at least the late 1960s.
  • Willem Van Lancker, Google Maps: Designing the Modern Atlas (~10 min: look at the pictures, and find the part about landmarks). One of the designers of Google Maps on how they decided what the map should show and what to leave out, and how they tried each new design on real people, one task at a time, before it was built. In Western countries people mostly find their way by street names; in much of Asia, including here, by landmarks and places — partly because Japanese street numbers follow the order in which buildings were put up, not their order along the street. So the map marks landmarks with icons, and Japan's map gets its own kinds of road. We talked about Google Maps in class as a product that works for the person using it; this is how the people who make it think about that.
  • Robin Sloan, An app can be a home-cooked meal (~7 min, 2020). He made a messaging app for his family. It has four users, and he calls that a resounding success. He calls himself the programming version of a home cook: someone who cooks for the people at their own table, not for a restaurant. Today you built a page for one person, and that counts. It is the course's kitchen again, at its smallest size.
  • Paul Graham, Do things that don't scale (~20 min, 2013). Advice for people starting a company, from a co-founder of Y Combinator, which funded Airbnb and Stripe when they were small. At the start, the founders did things by hand, one user at a time: Stripe's founders set new users up on the spot, on the user's own laptop, and Airbnb's went door to door in New York helping hosts. Today you did the same at the smallest size: one builder, one user, across a table. Some business words; the examples carry it.
