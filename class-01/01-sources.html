Class 1 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 1's claims, so that any of them can be checked: the history, the numbers, the bridges and the tools. A few are books, and each line says so. Some lines say where a source is more exact than what was said in class, or says something different.

The opening

  • Brad Bird and Jan Pinkava (directors), Ratatouille (Pixar Animation Studios, 2007) — Chef Gusteau's motto on Anyone can cook, which is also the title of his cookbook in the film, and Remy, the rat who does the cooking, on Meet your sous-chef. At the end of the film, the critic Anton Ego says what the motto means: not everyone can become a great artist, but a great artist can come from anywhere.
  • Japan, Act on the Protection of Personal Information (Act No. 57 of 2003; English translation by the Ministry of Justice) — the law behind Keep personal / sensitive data out of it on Working with AI. The English translation includes the changes to the law up to 2021, and only the Japanese text is the law itself. Its duties fall on businesses and public bodies, such as a university. In 2023 the Personal Information Protection Commission warned them that typing personal data into an AI service that learns from what it is given can count as handing the data to someone else (its leaflet, in Japanese).

How we got here

  • Ada Lovelace, Note G, in L. F. Menabrea, Sketch of the Analytical Engine Invented by Charles Babbage (translated with her notes, Taylor's Scientific Memoirs 3, 1843; the printed volume is on the Internet Archive) — the diagram on What do you think this is? and 1843 · the first program: her plan, one numbered operation at a time, for the engine to compute a Bernoulli number. Its columns are the number of the operation, what it does, the variables it acts on, and the result.
  • Stephen Wolfram, Untangling the Tale of Ada Lovelace (2015) — which number the diagram computes: the one Lovelace called B7, which is written B8 today and is −1/30, the answer the live spreadsheet reached. Wolfram also points out a mistake in the diagram: in operation 4, a fraction is upside down.
  • The Railroad Gazette, "Hollerith's Electric Tabulating Machine" (19 April 1895), pp. 246–247 — the punched card on the first rung of the ladder, Plugboards & switches, and where its picture comes from. The card records a freight shipment: a load of lumber sent north from one station to another. In class it was described as a record of people; the census card that did that is described in the same article, but not pictured. The article does show what the rung says: the machine's work was in its wiring, joined up at a switchboard.
  • R. F. Clippinger, A Logical Coding System Applied to the ENIAC (U.S. Army Ballistic Research Laboratories, Report No. 673, 1948) — ENIAC, on the same rung: changing the problem it worked on meant plugging many cables in and out, which took about a day. An Army history of 1961, Karl Kempf's Electronic Computers Within the Ordnance Corps, adds weeks of checking and setting up for each new problem.
  • Intel, Intel 64 and IA-32 Architectures Software Developer's Manuals, Volume 2 (free to download) — the bytes on the Machine code rung and the words for them on the Assembly rung. B8 and BB each put a number into a register, and 01 D8 adds one register to the other.
  • John Backus, The History of FORTRAN I, II, and III (ACM SIGPLAN Notices 13(8), 1978; posted with ACM's permission) — FORTRAN, on the High-level languages rung. In 1954 and 1955 the IBM customers Backus described it to were "mostly skeptical" that a machine could write programs as good as theirs. Two things said in class are less exact than his account. FORTRAN was not the first compiler: Backus himself calls Laning and Zierler's system, running at MIT in 1954, "the world's first operating algebraic compiler", and there were earlier compilers still. And it did not take most of a decade to become normal. Its compiler was finished in April 1957, and a year later more than half of the IBM 704 computer centres surveyed used it for more than half of their problems.
  • John Backus, Programming in America in the 1950s — Some Personal Impressions (in A History of Computing in the Twentieth Century, Academic Press, 1980) — the doubt behind That's not real programming, from somebody who was there. The early programmers, whom Backus calls "the priesthood", met plans to let more people program with hostility and derision.
  • The timeline on And when did each one become normal? is the instructor's own reading of when each way of working became ordinary, drawn as a shape rather than as exact years. Three of its starting points can be checked: the first program stored in a computer's memory ran in Manchester on 21 June 1948; Dennis Ritchie names 1972 as the most creative year of the C language; and FORTRAN's compiler was finished in April 1957.
  • Christopher Scaffidi, Mary Shaw and Brad Myers, Estimating the Numbers of End Users and End User Programmers (IEEE VL/HCC, 2005) — the 55 million spreadsheet and database users on How many people can build?, which is their projection, made in 2005, for American workplaces in 2012. They set it against fewer than 3 million professional programmers projected for the same year. The number they actually counted was over 45 million users, in 2001.
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Software Developers (Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May 2023) — the last point on the same chart: about 1.66 million software developers in 2023. The three earlier points on that line could not be traced to a published count. The Bureau's own older counts are 175,000 programmers in 1968 (Occupational Outlook Handbook, 1970–71), 230,000 in 1976 (its 1978–79 edition) and 648,000 in 1998 (Bulletin 2521), so the line rose earlier than the chart shows.
  • JetBrains, Global Developer Population Reaches 19.6 Million in 2024 (2025), and SlashData, There are 47.2 million developers in the world (2025) — two counts of the world's developers, and a real case of the question on What counts as programming?: JetBrains counts professional developers, and SlashData counts everyone who programs. SlashData's professionals alone, 36.5 million, are nearly twice JetBrains's number, so the way of counting changes the answer as much as the definition does.
  • Andy Gordon and Simon Peyton Jones, LAMBDA: The ultimate Excel worksheet function (Microsoft Research Blog, 2021) — Excel as a programming language, behind A spreadsheet formula computes, reacts, chains. It is Microsoft's own claim, with no data given, that Excel is "the world's most widely used programming language". In class it was put as the most widely used programming environment ever made.
  • The spreadsheet opened on the second What do you think this is? is the instructor's own, built from Lovelace's diagram.

Coding vs. building

  • American Society of Civil Engineers, Zhaozhou Bridge — the Anji Bridge on Build a bridge: finished in 605, under the Sui dynasty, designed and built by the mason Li Chun, and still in use. Photographs from 2024, on the Chinese government's English website, show visitors walking on it. It has carried no traffic since 1984, when a park was made around it (Xinhua, in Chinese).
  • New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission, The Arch and Colonnade of the Manhattan Bridge Approach (designation report LP-0899, 1975), p. 1 — the Manhattan Bridge on the same slide: formally opened to traffic on 31 December 1909. New York City's Department of Transportation counted about 70,000 vehicles, 3,400 people on foot and 6,400 cyclists crossing it on an average day in 2024 — how many cross it, on What drives it.
  • Titus Winters, "What Is Software Engineering?", chapter 1 of Titus Winters, Tom Manshreck and Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google (O'Reilly, 2020; free to read online) — the sentence Software engineering is programming integrated over time, which the chapter gives as something said inside Google. Three of the four lines on What drives it are the chapter's three differences between programming and software engineering, in plain words: Time and Change, Scale and Efficiency, and Trade-offs and Costs. The fourth, who has to look after it, is the course's own.
  • Adam Johnson, Software engineering is programming integrated over time (2021) — the picture on the two slides before the quote: a program as a square, and software engineering as the same square pulled along time into a solid. The idea is in Winters's chapter too: "Cubes aren't squares".
  • The kitchen on Building software is like running a kitchen and And kitchens exist at different scales is the course's own.
  • U.S. Geological Survey, API Documentation – Earthquake Catalog — the live earthquake data behind the app built on Why now?: public, with no key.
  • Alastair Tse, Fine Financials (9 July 2026) — the app on Fine: one messy, multi-country life, fully tracked, and the numbers on Who did the engineering?: about 20 days, 660 commits, and no code written by hand. One line on the slide is less exact than his post: the exchange rates are historical, not live.

Set up your workshop

  • iTerm2 — the terminal for macOS in Station 1.
  • Homebrew, brew.sh and Installation — the one line in Station 1, and the Command Line Tools it installs if they are missing. Since September 2026, a week after this class, Homebrew no longer supports Macs with Intel chips, and its installer stops on them.
  • Microsoft, Windows Terminal — the terminal for Windows in Station 1, and the default on Windows 11 since 2022. Microsoft's WinGet is the package manager that comes with Windows 10 and 11.
  • Anthropic, Advanced setup (Claude Code documentation) — the two install lines in Station 2, brew install --cask claude-code and winget install Anthropic.ClaudeCode. Claude's plans include Claude Code from Pro upward; the free plan does not.
  • Scott Chacon and Ben Straub, Pro Git (Apress, second edition; CC BY-NC-SA 3.0), section 1.1 — Git, in Station 3. Version control is "a system that records changes to a file or set of files over time", which the class called a save system that remembers every version.
  • GitHub, What is GitHub Pages? (GitHub Docs) — the publishing in Station 4.
  • Visual Studio Code, the Claude desktop app and Codex — the three tools on Optional · A window instead of a terminal.
