Class 2 — Sources

Not homework. These are the sources behind Class 2's claims, so that any of them can be checked. Most of this class came from practice — a page built, published and opened on somebody else's phone, in the room — so most of this list is the tools' own documentation. Some lines say where a source is more exact than what was said in class.

Pick

  • Randall Munroe, Tasks (xkcd 1425, 24 September 2014, CC BY-NC 2.5) — the comic on its own slide, and again in the middle of A photo app · someone asks for two things: checking that a photo was taken in a national park is a few hours' work, and checking that it shows a bird needs "a research team and five years".
  • P. Jonathon Phillips and colleagues, Face Recognition Vendor Test 2002: Evaluation Report (U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, NISTIR 6965, March 2003) — the 2002 panel. The U.S. government tested that year's face-recognition systems, and the report concluded that recognising faces in photos taken outdoors still needed research.
  • Apple, Recognizing People in Photos Through Private On-Device Machine Learning (Apple Machine Learning Research, 2021) — why the 2002 request was hard, from the people who later put the answer on a phone: in the photos people take, a face can be any size, in any light, at any angle. Apple's own Find People and Pets in Photos on your iPhone or iPad is the button it became.
  • Google, Picture this: A fresh approach to Photos (2015) — the same button on the other kind of phone: Google Photos sorts your pictures by the people in them, and nobody tags anything.
  • Flickr, Introducing: Flickr PARK or BIRD (Flickr's engineering blog, 20 October 2014) — what came after the comic: 26 days later, Flickr built both checks. The bird check used a network its vision team had been teaching for about a year to recognise more than a thousand kinds of thing — a research team, as the comic said.
  • Hugging Face, Pipelines (Transformers documentation) — now: one line of code. The documentation's own example names the bird in a photo in three lines: one to load the library, one to load a model, and one to ask. "One line" is the short way to say it. The same page writes a caption for a photo in the same few lines, which is the easy half of the 2026 panel.
  • Hany Farid, Mitigating the harms of manipulated media: Confronting deepfakes and digital deception (PNAS Nexus 4(7), 2025; free to read) — still unsolved, on the 2026 panel. A forensic check can sometimes prove that a photo was changed, but when it finds nothing, that does not prove the photo is real.
  • Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, C2PA Explainer (version 2.4) — the partial answer that does exist. A camera can sign a photo and record each edit made to it, and some new phones, such as Google's Pixel 10, do this for every photo. The record is optional and can be removed, so it answers the question only for a photo that still carries it.
  • Open-Meteo, Weather Forecast API — the weather row on Pick one: free for non-commercial use, with no key and no account. Its data is licensed CC BY 4.0, which asks for a link to Open-Meteo wherever the data is shown.

Build

  • Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie, UNIX Programmer's Manual (first edition, Bell Labs, 3 November 1971) — the oldest home of the names on They came with the terminal: cat "concatenate (or print) files", ls "list contents of directory", mkdir "create directory", and chdir, which later became cd. pwd and grep came later; the GNU Coreutils manual spells pwd out as "print working directory".
  • M. Douglas McIlroy, A Research UNIX Reader (Bell Labs, Computing Science Technical Report 139, 1987), p. 8 — where grep came from. Ken Thompson took the pattern-matching part out of the editor ed, so that a file too big for the editor could still be searched, and named the new program after the editor's command g/re/p: search globally for a regular expression, and print the lines that match. Brian Kernighan tells the story in Where GREP Came From (Computerphile, 2018).
  • Why the names are short. In class, the names were said to be short because people were "charged by the character". The sources do not say that. Multics, the system Unix's authors had worked on before, gave each command a long name and a short one — list and ls, copy and cp — and Unix kept the short ones. And on Bell Labs' Unix machine, Lorinda Cherry remembered, "there was no charging" at all (interviewed by Michael S. Mahoney, 1989).
  • Git, Reference — the official pages for the five commands on These are installed: clone, status, add, commit and push.
  • Git, gitignore — the file on .gitignore — the things git should skip: each line is a pattern, and git leaves out the files that match it. A file saved before its name went on the list stays saved. GitHub's own gitignore templates list .DS_Store for macOS and Thumbs.db for Windows.

Ship

  • GitHub, Creating a GitHub Pages site (GitHub Docs) — the rows on Potential problems. GitHub Pages looks for index.html, index.md or README.md at the top of the folder it publishes, which is why a page kept in a sub-folder shows the README instead. A site that never appears needs a push from somebody with a verified email address. And on GitHub Free, the repository has to be public. In class, the wait after a first publish was two minutes; GitHub's own figure is up to ten.
